ZZepto RCA Problem framing

Closing the average order value gap

Zepto sits ₹157 behind the category leader on average order value. The gap is not demand and it is not brand. It is a stretch of the price range in which the product gives a shopper no reason at all to add one more thing.

₹540Zepto average order valueAgainst ₹660 at Blinkit and ₹697 at Instamart
63%Stop adding items at the free delivery lineThe threshold acts as a finish line
77%Of surveyed orders come in under ₹500Well below the platform average
₹149 to ₹1,000The range in which no incentive existsWhere almost every order actually lands

The problem

Speed is the moat, and also the ceiling

Quick commerce economics are unforgiving. Every order carries a fixed cost in picking, packing and rider time, so a platform delivering a ₹540 basket in ten minutes earns less per delivery than one moving ₹697. Zepto's average order value has stayed flat while both competitors grew.

Home, search and cart are all built for speed to checkout rather than basket to completion. Once a shopper finds the item they came for, nothing in the product asks them to continue, so the session ends exactly where the urgent need ended.

QAre we growing average order value, or growing revenue?
AAverage order value, without losing order volume. Raising the basket by pushing away small urgent orders reads as a win on the headline number and a loss on the business, so order volume becomes a guardrail rather than a target.
QIs the ten minute promise negotiable?
ANo, in which case every proposal has to fit inside it. A bigger basket takes longer to pick, so delivery time becomes a second guardrail rather than an acceptable trade.

The approach · Market and user research

What we did to find the answer

Two inputs. First, a read of what the competition publishes, because in this category they say a great deal. Instamart attributes a 14% quarterly rise in average order value directly to Maxxsaver, its basket building mechanic, which is close to a stated answer. Second, a primary survey of 30 shoppers across Pune, Bengaluru, Chennai and Mumbai, which returned a midpoint basket of ₹414 and confirmed the gap is structural rather than seasonal.

The survey agreed on three things. The free delivery line is treated as a finish line, with 63% stopping at or near it and 40% stopping after a single item. Discovery is too generic to pull anyone sideways, with 43% saying they rarely try new categories. And the forgotten item only surfaces after payment, at which point the product has already closed the door.

Clustering the responses produced five behavioural segments, of which three are already responsive to basket building mechanics, together 67% of the sample. The read worth keeping is that the highest value segment does almost no discovery beyond the products it already buys, so the ceiling on Zepto's best shoppers is set entirely by discovery rather than by loyalty.

The diagnosis

Plotting the incentive structure against where orders actually land makes the problem obvious. Zepto rewards a shopper at ₹149 and then again at ₹1,000, and does nothing at all in between.

The reframe

The right question is not "how do we make shoppers browse for longer", because that attacks the moat. It is "how do we deepen baskets without breaking the ten minute promise", which is a question the product can actually answer.

The solutions

Five mechanics, each removing one blocker

MechanicWhat it doesBlocker it removes
Basket ladder Four visible slabs at ₹149, ₹299, ₹499 and ₹799, so a next step always exists. Collapsed for urgent sessions and expanded for browse sessions. The ₹149 to ₹1,000 dead zone
Pre cart discovery Intent routed cards on home, category and product pages. Recipe completion, adjacent category prompts, and a forgotten item widget. Discovery too generic to pull anyone sideways
Cart layer Three transparent nudges in the cart: an always on value line, real event based scarcity, and a standard offers lane. The cart behaves like a receipt, not a shelf
Post cart recovery A 60 to 120 second window after checkout asking whether anything was forgotten, with one tap adds while the delivery slot is still held. No way back in after payment
Intent ranked pairs Frequently bought together pairs on product and cart pages, ranked by current intent rather than by overall popularity. Rewards attach to items, never to the basket

The basket ladder ships first, and not because it scores well. It is the layer every other mechanic points at. Discovery, cart nudges and recovery all need a visible next step to exist before they have anything to say.

Design constraint

No dark patterns and no fabricated urgency. Scarcity messaging is only used where a real event backs it, because a quick commerce shopper who catches the product lying once has very little switching cost.

The prototype

What the app looks like once this ships

The same cart, the same two items, the same ₹312. The only thing that changes is whether the screen gives the shopper somewhere to go next.

Open prototype

How we would know

Three numbers, three guardrails

Segment targeted exposure across three cities with a matched in segment control rather than a blanket split, because baskets range from ₹311 to ₹575 across the five segments and random assignment would let segment mix masquerade as a treatment effect.

The test is powered to detect a ₹20 lift, ships on a ₹30 lift with no guardrail breach, and is killed by two consecutive guardrail failures or a negative primary metric at day 14. The phase one target is a population average order value of ₹575 to ₹595.

Takeaway

Protect the ten minute moat, but give the shopper a reason to keep building once the first need is solved. The gap to the category leader is not a discount the product forgot to offer. It is 850 rupees of silence in the middle of the price range, and the fix is simply to put something there.

Attribution

This was submitted as a group assignment. The framing, research synthesis, prioritisation and experiment design presented here are the parts I worked on and can defend in detail.

ZZomato Metrics OKRs and funnel analysis

The OKR that survives contact with a funnel

Per 100 Tier 1 app opens, only 12 end in a delivered order. The useful discovery is that the leaks above the cart and the leaks below it are different kinds of problem, owned by different teams and fixed by different work.

100 → 12App opens that reach a delivered orderTwo problem types wearing one funnel
WAEWeekly Active Eaters, the north starIt factors cleanly into all four key results
6 → 2Cohorts collapsed into two recurring patternsThe synthesis that makes a roadmap possible
6Guardrails built to stop the north star being gamedMargin, spend, riders, partners, trust

The problem

Which number should a business at this scale organise a year around?

Zomato is not short of users or awareness. The interesting question is not growth in the abstract. It is which single number the company should point a year at, and whether that number can be moved without quietly breaking something else.

QWhat counts as an active user?
AOnly a successfully delivered order. Counting an order that arrived cold or late as engagement rewards exactly the behaviour the strategy is trying to remove.
QWhy weekly rather than daily or monthly?
ADaily over indexes on a power user tail that flatters the number. Monthly hides churn for four weeks at a time. Weekly is where a real habit forms, in which a restaurant becomes a regular and a lunch becomes a routine.

Become the go to weekly meal companion for Tier 1 urban India, in which users discover effortlessly, order in seconds, and receive on time, every single time.

The CY2026 objective, with every word doing work

The approach · The north star and the funnel

Three lenses, forced to reconcile

A funnel shows where users leak inside a single session. A cohort shows who leaks over months and why. Neither tells you what to do. Objectives and key results are the operational answer that ties the two together, and the discipline was that every key result had to trace back to a specific funnel leak or a specific cohort drop.

The approach · Experiment design

Each leak gets one isolated experiment

Bundled interventions produce results nobody can attribute. Every stage above the cart gets a single change tested against a named control, and the delivery stage gets three separate experiments rather than one.

StageWhat changesPrimary metricTarget
1 to 2Personalised top three picks above the fold, against a generic restaurant listSessions engaging discovery within 10 seconds75 → 85%
2 to 3A contextual cue on the top three to five cards only, against standard cardsCard to menu click through60 → 75%
3 to 4A most ordered here carousel pinned above the category listAdd to cart conversion49 → 62%
4 to 5All in pricing from the first item with the best coupon applied visiblyCarts reaching checkout82 → 92%
5 to 6Single screen checkout with a context aware address prefilledCheckout completion and time to order78 → 90%

The delivery stage is three experiments, not one

Test A

Predictive arrival time

A user level trial at 10%. A learned estimate using traffic, kitchen load and rider availability, against the current heuristic.

Orders within five minutes of the promise. Guardrails on refund cost and satisfaction.

Test B

Insulated delivery

A geographic cluster trial in two pilot zones, because equipping riders is a rider level change rather than a user level one.

Cold food complaints per thousand orders. Guardrails on equipment cost and compliance.

Test C

Refund before the complaint

An order level trial at 20%. An automatic refund when a delay beyond ten minutes is predicted, rather than waiting to be asked.

Issues resolved before the user reports them. Guardrails on refund cost and net promoter score.

Why test B is designed differently

A rider carries orders for many users, so randomising insulated bags per user leaks the treatment into the control group and the result means nothing. Cluster randomisation by geography is the only design that gives a clean read. Getting this wrong is one of the more common ways an experiment programme produces confident nonsense.

The approach · Cohorts

Read the curve shape before proposing anything

The funnel says nothing about who leaks over months. Before writing a single cohort intervention I classified each retention curve by shape, because the shape decides whether the right move is to fix onboarding, invest in winning people back, or stop spending on that group entirely.

Six cohorts collapse into two patterns

Six separate cohort programmes is a roadmap nobody can staff. Collapsing them produced something more useful than any single intervention, which is that retention here is not a discount problem at all.

Pattern 1

Make the use case explicit

Low frequency, single orderer and dinner dominant users churn because the app hands them a generic experience built for the median customer.

Fix: named, occasion anchored experiences. Weekday lunch, made for one, Friday night, Sunday treat.

People hire this product for a job, and "feed me on a busy weekday" is a different job from "treat me on Friday". One generic feed serves neither.

Pattern 2

Turn episodic into durable

New users, discount driven users and subscribers all churn at transition moments. The end of the honeymoon, the end of the promotion, the renewal date.

Fix: replace the episodic high with a durable reason. Habit, everyday value, visible ongoing value.

Episodic engagement is sugar. It spikes and then crashes. A no surge lunch at a fair price is durable. A flash sale is not.

The solutions · The fixes on screen

What the two largest leaks look like once they are fixed

Both of the biggest drops happen before anyone reaches a cart, which means they are decision problems. These are the screens that carry the fixes, plus the delivery moment that turns a complaint into a resolved issue.

Open prototype

Guardrails

Hitting the north star through unhealthy means is failure

Weekly Active Eaters is gameable. You can buy it with discounts, buy it with advertising, or extract it from riders and restaurant partners. Each of those wins the metric and loses the business, so each one gets a named threshold agreed before anything ships. The six cover contribution margin per order, acquisition cost, rider earnings per hour, restaurant partner churn, push notification opt outs, and app store trust.

The one worth arguing hardest for

Notification hygiene. Three of the twelve initiatives ship into the same push channel, and individually each looks reasonable. Together they can drive an opt out cascade that permanently removes a surface the company cannot buy back. That risk is invisible unless somebody looks across initiatives rather than down at one.

Takeaway

A funnel exposes where users leak in the moment. A cohort exposes who leaks over months and why. Objectives and key results are the operational answer to both. Every key result here traces back to a named leak or a named drop, every initiative is measured as an outcome rather than an activity count, and every hypothesis is written as an experiment somebody could actually run on Monday.

Stated assumptions

Every baseline is an illustrative public benchmark drawn from investor disclosures, the annual report and published industry estimates, pending replacement with internal data on the first day of execution. Tier 1 is treated as a single market, although Bangalore, Mumbai and Delhi differ materially in density and competitive intensity and a real programme would separate them. Sample sizes would be set from observed variance rather than assumed.

I would rather present a strategy that names what it does not know than one that quietly launders industry estimates into the language of internal truth.

UUber India Product improvement User research

The last 200 meters

Riders have quietly built a coordination system outside the product. 78% of them phone the driver, because the app cannot reliably close the final stretch between a map pin and a person standing on a street.

73%Face difficulty finding their rideOnly 1 of 41 riders said this never happens
78%Phone the driver as fallback navigationA workaround this common is a product gap
87%Report negative emotions at pickupAnxiety, frustration, exhaustion, fear
83%Would accept a suggested pickup spotThe fix does not require behaviour change

The problem

Find a real user problem, then prove with research that it is real.

I chose the pickup moment because it is the one point at which a ride hailing product hands off to the physical world, and handoffs are usually where things go wrong. It is also the rare problem that is entirely controllable. Traffic is not, fuel prices are not, and competitor pricing is not, but the last 200 meters sits fully inside Uber's own product.

The commercial case for looking here is straightforward. Uber India completes roughly 62% of bookings against an industry norm nearer 80%, and 82% of riders had a cancellation in the past year. A cancellation is not a neutral event, because it costs a fare, wastes a driver trip, and leaves a rider one tap away from an alternative app.

The approach · Clarifying questions

Scoping the question

QIs this a rider problem or a driver problem?
ABoth sides lose, although only the rider can churn to another app in the same minute. I scoped the research to riders and treated driver time as a benefit rather than the primary lens.
QWhich market are we designing for?
ATier 1 India, in which addresses are frequently informal, landmarks matter more than street names, and rider and driver often do not share a first language.
QAre we solving navigation or trust?
AThe research answered this rather than my assumptions. Navigation is the visible failure, but the loudest complaint turned out to be extra fare demands, which is a trust failure occurring at the same moment.
QWhat counts as success?
AA measurable fall in pickup driven cancellations, since that is the point at which rider frustration becomes lost revenue.

The approach · Market and user research

Two layers, because numbers and stories fail differently

A survey tells you how often something happens, but it cannot tell you how it feels and it cannot surface the thing you forgot to ask about. So the survey ran first in order to establish pattern and scale, and the interviews ran afterwards in order to explain the patterns it found.

Sample

Chennai 66% and Bengaluru 27%. Rapido was the most used app at 46% with Uber second at 29%, which matters because most respondents were describing Uber comparatively rather than as loyalists. Autos accounted for 63% of bookings. The mix covered students aged 19 to 24 and professionals aged 25 to 35, across both daily commuters and occasional riders.

The approach · What riders do today

Riders built a coordination system outside the product

The most useful question in the survey was not about satisfaction. It was what riders do when the driver cannot find them, because the workaround people invent tells you precisely which job the product failed to complete.

"I usually cancel and use Rapido. By the time Uber is looking for a driver, Rapido confirms."

Ashley, Chennai

The approach · Personas

Four personas, clustered from behaviour rather than demography

About 40%

The budget optimizer

Student, 19 to 24, Chennai. Rapido and autos, price first.

Primary pain: extra money demands, and cancellations at peak hours.

About 30%

The daily commuter

IT professional, 24 to 32, Bengaluru. Uber and Rapido, reliability first.

Primary pain: tech park gates, and drivers unfamiliar with campus layouts.

About 20%

The anxious navigator

Woman, 22 to 35, travelling across unfamiliar cities. Uber and Ola, safety first.

Primary pain: language barriers, personal safety, and GPS drift.

About 10%

The resigned accepter

Infrequent rider, uses whatever is available, expectations already lowered.

Primary pain: learned helplessness, summarised as "this is just how it is".

The one to watch

The resigned accepter is the persona that should worry a product manager most. They do not complain, do not churn loudly, and never appear in support tickets. They simply stop expecting better, which means every satisfaction metric reads acceptably right up until the moment they leave.

The solutions

Three proposals, each tied to the finding that justifies it

I held every proposal to one rule, which is that it must cite the specific research finding that makes it necessary. An idea that cannot name its evidence is only a preference.

The visual · The journey, before and after

What the pickup feels like now, and what it could feel like

Placing the findings in sequence shows that the damage is cumulative. No single stage is catastrophic on its own, but confidence falls at every step until the rider arrives at the car already annoyed, which is the state in which a fare dispute turns into a churn event.

The same journey, on the ground

The curve above is the feeling. This is the geometry underneath it. A residential colony is the most cited failure venue in the survey, so it is the one worth drawing.

Takeaway

The thesis

Uber India does not have a pricing problem. It has a pickup experience problem that presents itself as a pricing problem.

When asked what they would change, a number of riders answered some version of "nothing, just make it cheapest". The easy reading is that this market is purely price sensitive and the product barely matters.

I think that reading is wrong. A rider who says "nothing, cheapest" is not stating a price preference, but rather the absence of a reason to pay more. Every premium tier tried in this market has run into that sentence, because the product has never reliably delivered the one thing riders would actually pay for, which is the ride arriving where they are standing.

The market model behind the ₹165 crore figure is worked through separately in Sizing the pickup problem.

Where I would push back on myself

The sample skews 93% South India and over represents Rapido users, so the competitive dynamics described hold most strongly for Chennai and Bengaluru. Before funding any of this I would want a matched read against internal cancellation logs broken down by venue type, since that is the data most likely to confirm or kill the smart spot proposal quickly.

UUber Eats Product improvement Jobs to be done

Why groups abandon Group Order for a WhatsApp poll

Group Order is hired to do three jobs: coordinate the group, prove the split is fair, and make ordering feel social. It falls short on all three, so groups return to the tool that at least handles the first one.

The problem

Uber Eats Group Order exists and works. Why does almost nobody use it?

The feature ships, the flow completes, and adoption is still low. That gap is what makes the problem worth looking at. A feature that functions but is not being hired is failing at the level of the job rather than the level of the build. So I worked backwards from the job instead of forwards from the screen.

The approach · Clarifying questions

Scoping the question

QAre we solving for the host or for the participants?
ABoth, although they are different users with opposing needs. The host wants control, while participants want to be left alone. Most of the failure sits inside that tension.
QWhat is Group Order actually competing against?
ANot Swiggy or Zomato. It competes with a WhatsApp poll plus one person paying and then chasing reimbursements. That is the real incumbent, and it is free.
QWhich market are we designing for?
AIndia, in which app install dependency becomes a first order constraint rather than an edge case.
QIs this a discovery problem or an experience problem?
AAn experience problem. People who try it once do not come back, which is not how a discovery problem behaves.

The approach · The jobs users hire it for

What users hire Group Order to do

There are three layers of job here, and the feature is judged on all three at once.

Functional

Get the order placed

  • Coordinate diverse food preferences
  • Split the bill fairly
  • One delivery, one shared fee
  • Handle bulk orders for events
  • Manage the checkout timer

Emotional

Feel in control, not exposed

  • Feel organised as the host
  • Keep my food choices private
  • Trust the payment maths
  • Know the order is actually locked

Social

Make it worth doing together

  • Build team camaraderie
  • Be seen as a thoughtful host
  • Make ordering fun rather than a chore

The insight

Group Order is hired for coordination, trust and social bonding rather than for convenience. Convenience is what a solo order sells. If the feature falls short on any one of the three, the group returns to WhatsApp, which does not promise to solve the other two in the first place.

The approach · From gaps to guarantees

From jobs to features, without skipping a step

Most feature lists move straight from complaint to solution. This chain keeps every feature traceable: it exists because a guarantee requires it, the guarantee exists because a root cause requires it, and the root cause was found by interrogating a specific failed job.

The visual · The user journey

Where the group actually drops out

The six anxieties are easier to act on once they are placed in order. Mapping the journey from the first suggestion through to settling up shows two points at which groups give up and go back to WhatsApp, and both of them sit before the food is even ordered.

Open prototype

What the journey shows

The group never reaches the part of the experience Uber Eats is good at. They leave at Join, in which the install requirement blocks half the team, and they leave again at Checkout, in which nobody can see what they owe. By the time delivery quality or restaurant range matters, the group has already gone back to a WhatsApp thread.

Takeaway

The feature was not under built. It was under specified. Nobody had written down what a group order must guarantee, so each release added capability without closing the trust gap that sends groups back to WhatsApp. Five guarantees, derived from first principles, turn an open ended feature backlog into a pass or fail test.

GGarmin Connect Product design UX evaluation

A ninety second check in that takes five minutes

Garmin Connect holds more accurate data than almost any competitor and presents it worse than most. I walked one everyday flow, logged twelve usability issues across ten heuristics, and then rebuilt the interface with every fix applied.

12Usability issues in a single daily flowAcross five screens and about ninety seconds
10 / 10Nielsen heuristics touchedEvery one of them violated at least once
8 monthsStale date range shown on the sleep screenPresented without any indication it was old
6 → 3Metric cards after the redesignGrouped rather than deleted

The problem

The data is right. The reading of it is the product.

The user is a fitness enthusiast who trains four to six times a week on a Forerunner and cares about trends, recovery and personal records. The flow is the one they run every day: open the app after a workout, see today, check sleep, review the activity, and glance at what is coming.

"I want to see how I performed today, slept last night, and what is next, without digging through menus."

The user story the flow was evaluated against

Five screens, roughly ninety seconds of intent. In practice it takes far longer, because at almost every step the screen shows a number without telling you what the number means, how recent it is, or what good would look like.

The approach

What the walkthrough found

Twelve issues, each mapped to the heuristic it violates and the fix it needs. They fall into four groups, and the grouping is what turned a defect list into a redesign.

Group 1

Data that is stale or unlabelled

The sleep screen opens on a four week range ending eight months in the past, with nothing marking it as old. The heart rate gauge reads two dashes while the line underneath says 70 bpm resting, so the reader cannot tell whether it is live, stale, or a summary.

Violates: visibility of system status, match with the real world

Group 2

Data that is simply wrong

One continuous workout is auto split into three separate Running entries of 0.21, 1.07 and 2.26 km. The first of those is logged at 14:36 per km, which is a walking pace being recorded as a run.

Violates: error prevention, user control and freedom

Group 3

Numbers with no scale or key

Stress reads 89 with no indication of the range or whether high is bad. The calendar colours dates with six different bars and offers no legend. The Body Battery chart plots three series with nothing identifying any of them.

Violates: recognition rather than recall, help and documentation

Group 4

Jargon and density

Six metric cards sit on one screen, past the limit of what anyone holds in working memory. They are labelled Body Battery, Fitness Age and Intensity Minutes with no explanation. The top bar carries five unlabelled glyphs, and expedition distances mix units across 696lm, 260K, 3500K and 784K.

Violates: aesthetic and minimalist design, consistency and standards

Where it breaks the laws of UX

LawVerdictWhere it shows up
Miller's lawViolatedSix metric cards on one screen, brushing the seven plus or minus two working memory limit.
Hick's lawViolatedFive navigation tabs, six cards and five home sections together produce decision overload.
Goal gradientViolatedSleep data eight months stale means there is no current progress feedback toward any goal.
Information scentWeakLabels like Body Battery and Fitness Age give no preview of what tapping them reveals.
Aesthetic usabilityWeakDense grey on grey cards read as utilitarian next to the polish of Strava or Whoop.
Jakob's lawWeakPlatform conventions are broken through the missing calendar legend, inconsistent units and non standard glyph navigation.

The solutions

Six cards become three, and every number gains a meaning

The instinct with a crowded screen is to delete things. That is the wrong move here, because the data is the reason people buy the watch. The fix is to group it, name it in plain language, and give every number the context that makes it readable at a glance, which is the pattern Strava gets right and Garmin does not.

The rest of the flow

The same three rules carry through the other screens. Correct the data before displaying it, never show a value without its scale, and say in words what the colours mean.

Open prototype

Takeaway

Garmin's problem is not the data and it is not the hardware. It is that the app presents measurements where the user needs meaning. A resting heart rate of 70 is a number. Seventy, inside your normal range of 66 to 74, with lower usually meaning better recovery, is an answer. The second one takes the same space on screen.

The three rules that produced every screen above are worth stating plainly, because they generalise well beyond this app. Correct the data before you display it. Never show a value without the scale that makes it readable. And say in words what the colours mean, rather than expecting anyone to learn a key that was never provided.

VVitaFit Product design PRD

The long game

A fitness platform rotates its trainers, so nobody remembers your injury and you explain yourself again every session. Its leaderboards put a 45 year old homemaker against a 25 year old athlete. Both problems have the same effect, which is that people quietly stop booking.

18%Day 30 retentionAgainst an industry benchmark of 30 to 35%
45%Fall in repeat session rate from week 1 to week 4The drop happens inside the first month
Under 5%Trainer favouriting rateAlmost nobody forms a bond with a coach
ZeroOffline community presenceWhile the category leader runs events every weekend

The problem

Two failures that produce the same silence

Failure 1

Motivation fades

A single open leaderboard means most people are permanently near the bottom of it. A 45 year old homemaker cannot compete with a 25 year old athlete, so the feature that is supposed to motivate her is the one telling her she does not belong. Progress becomes invisible, and invisible progress stops being pursued.

Failure 2

No bond with a trainer

Trainers rotate, so Trainer A has no idea what Trainer B taught. The user re-explains their injury, their goal and their limits every single session. What should be a relationship reads as a transaction, and a transaction has nothing holding it together.

"The trainer did not know about my injury." "It felt impersonal." "I quit after my favourite trainer left." "The platform feels too automated."

Customer support verbatims, quoted in the requirements document

The comparison that made the case internally was Nike Run Club, which sustains retention above 80% on streaks and coaching alone. The gap is not effort from users. It is that nothing in the product remembers them between sessions.

The approach · The competitive gap

Every competitor owns one dimension, and nobody owns all three

Cult.fit has proven that offline community works at scale in India, but its classes are not coach bonded. Strava has the stickiest competition mechanics in fitness, but open leaderboards actively demotivate casual users and it has no trainer layer at all. HealthifyMe leads on AI nutrition, but its coach chat is transactional and it runs no events.

The solutions

Two features, written for the teams that have to build them

Feature A

Coach Connect

A Fitness Passport that travels with the user across rotating trainers. The user logs meals, weight, mood and energy. The trainer adds session notes, weekly recommendations and goal adjustments. Medical history is always visible to the trainer, and the user controls every non medical field.

An AI handoff turns recent sessions and the shared document into a thirty second brief the next trainer reads before walking over. This is not automation replacing a coach, it is a coach arriving already informed.

Sub cohort leaderboards bucket users automatically by age, weight, gender and discipline, so ranking happens against genuine peers. Streaks, XP and brand partner rewards sit on top, with a flexible freeze so one missed day does not erase a month.

Feature B

Tribes and Chapters

Squad challenges run online through deep link invites, with a live leaderboard and a group chat. Family rivalry and peer cheering do the work that a notification cannot.

Regional chapters run offline, suggested by pin code, covering runs, pickleball, paddle and yoga workshops. They are trainer led, capacity capped, and bookable in the app.

The two halves are joined deliberately. Completing a set of online sessions unlocks event tickets, and the event produces photos and recaps that pull the next group of people back online.

The prototype

What design and engineering actually build

A requirements document that stops at prose leaves every hard decision to whoever reads it last. These are the four screens that carry the two features, drawn so the team can argue with them.

Phase zero builds nothing. The Fitness Passport runs as a manual shared document with a handful of power users and their trainers, because if people will not fill it in by hand they will not fill it in inside an app either.

Phase 0

Phase 2

Full rollout on proven improvement, opened with a marquee offline event.

UUber India Guesstimates Market sizing

Sizing the pickup problem

Every product proposal eventually meets the question of whether it is worth doing. This is the arithmetic behind a claim of ₹165 crore a year, built two separate ways so the two can be checked against each other.

₹12,400 CrTotal market affected by pickup frictionAll ride hailing in India, per year
₹3,720 CrThe share of that sitting inside one operatorServiceable available market
₹165 CrRealistically recoverable in twelve monthsSix Tier 1 cities
₹69 to ₹345 CrThe honest range once assumptions are stressedA factor of five, which is worth saying out loud

The question

How much money is actually at stake in fixing ride hailing pickup in India?

This estimate sits underneath a research led product case, in which 73% of riders reported difficulty finding their driver and 17% said they cancel and switch app when it happens. The research established that the problem is real. This is the separate question of whether it is large enough to fund.

QWhat exactly are we sizing?
ARevenue at risk rather than total revenue. A ride that completes despite a painful pickup still earns money today, so counting the whole market would be dishonest. The relevant number is the share of bookings that are lost or degraded because of pickup.
QOver what period?
AAnnual, and recoverable within twelve months of shipping. A number that only arrives in year four cannot be used to argue for headcount now.
QTop down or bottom up?
ABoth, deliberately. A single chain of multiplications is impossible to sanity check, because there is nothing to compare the answer against. Two independent routes give you one.

Assumptions

Stated first, so they can be attacked

The estimate is only as good as these five inputs, and the honest thing is to list them before the arithmetic rather than bury them in a footnote.

InputValue usedWhere it comes from
India ride hailing market8.28 billion dollarsPublished market estimate, 11.3% compound growth, 19.2% penetration
Exchange rate₹84 to the dollarAssumed. Moves the whole answer proportionally
Share of bookings affected18%Anchored on the 17% of surveyed riders who cancel and switch, rounded up slightly for rides that complete but degrade
Operator market share30%Assumed for the operator in question
Six Tier 1 cities55% of bookingsAssumed concentration of demand in the largest metros
Recoverable in year one8%The weakest assumption in the chain, and the one the sensitivity below leans on hardest

Route one

Top down, from the market inwards

Route two

Bottom up, from rides outwards

The second route never touches the published market figure. It starts from how many rides happen and what one costs, which means it can fail independently, and that is the entire point of running it.

What the gap tells you

The bottom up route lands lower, which is what you would expect, since the average fare of ₹180 reflects an auto heavy mix and the published market figure includes higher value segments. The two disagreeing by a quarter is useful information. Two routes agreeing exactly would suggest one of them was quietly derived from the other.

Stress test

What the answer looks like when the assumptions are wrong

A single number invites false confidence. Flexing the two weakest inputs, the share of bookings affected and the share recoverable in year one, produces a range rather than a point.

That last line is the useful one. The point of a sizing is rarely the number itself. It is whether the decision changes across the plausible range. Here it does not, since even the pessimistic case comfortably funds the work, which means the argument can move on from whether to build to what to build first.

Takeaway

A guesstimate is not a prediction. It is a structured argument with every joint exposed, so that anyone who disagrees can point at the specific assumption they would change rather than rejecting the conclusion wholesale. Three habits carry it: state the inputs before the arithmetic, build it twice by independent routes, and publish the range rather than the point.

Honesty

Every figure here is built from published market estimates and a survey of 41 riders, not from any operator's internal data. The headline results of ₹12,400 crore, ₹3,720 crore and ₹165 crore come from the original analysis. The step by step derivation, the bottom up cross check and the sensitivity range were reconstructed to show the method, so treat the working as an illustration of how the number can be defended rather than as a company forecast.

MMIKIDS · Edtech product Program Program delivery and retention

Product development

Everything else on this site is a case study. This one shipped. Program delivery and retention for MIKIDS across 500 plus partner schools in 8 states, covering the retention turnaround, a record year of new business, and the tooling built to keep the numbers honest.

26% → ~20%School dropout, containedStated conservatively, including post cycle book returns
₹3 Cr+New business in a single yearA first in the 20 year history of MIKIDS
241Schools running MIKIDS ScienceFrom needs assessment to deployment
1,200+Teachers in the feedback systemCovering 80% plus of partner schools

The problem

A quarter of the schools were leaving every year

MIKIDS is an edtech program running inside 500 plus partner schools across 8 states. When I took over retention, 26% of schools were dropping out annually, which put ₹1.4 crore of revenue at risk and meant field teams spent their year re selling to replacement schools instead of deepening the ones they had.

Nobody knew precisely why schools left, because nobody was asking the schools that had already gone. The renewal conversation happened at renewal time, which is the one moment a school that has decided to leave has no reason to be honest with you.

The approach

Ask the schools that left, then build for the ones that stayed

The same sequence the case studies on this site follow, run for real. Offboarding research with dropped out schools to learn the actual reasons, demographic impact studies across boards and urban rural splits in 7 regions, and region wise work plans built from the evidence. The findings became a retention playbook that leadership adopted, and a multi channel feedback system now covers 1,200 plus teachers, so problems surface while they are still fixable.

Dropout came down from 26% to roughly 20%. I am stating that conservatively rather than quoting the best in year figure, because book returns and late dropouts land after the annual number is first cut, and a retention figure that ignores them flatters itself.

What shipped

The record year, and how it was built

New business crossed ₹3 crore in a single year, which had not happened in the 20 years MIKIDS has existed. That number was not a sales spike. It came from the same machinery as the retention work: MIKIDS Science giving existing schools a reason to expand, requirements synthesised from 1,200 plus teacher inputs so the program fit classrooms rather than brochures, and the Orator launch opening a new line with ₹40 lakh plus projected from the first 100 schools.

MIKIDS Science

A 40 lesson curriculum program taken from needs assessment to live deployment in 241 schools across 8 states.

Orator

A Class 4 communication skills program coordinated end to end across content, design and engineering.

The retention playbook

Offboarding research and demographic studies distilled into a playbook leadership adopted, with a feedback system reaching 80% plus of partner schools.

The results

What the year actually produced

  • A record ₹3 crore plus of new business in a single year, the first time in the program's 20 year history
  • School dropout contained from 26% to about 20%, with the retention playbook adopted by leadership
  • Orator launched as a whole new business line, coordinated across content, design and engineering, with ₹40 lakh plus projected from the first 100 schools
  • Monetisation of existing schools worked: a ₹54.5 lakh upsell pipeline built from schools already running the program, at near zero acquisition cost
  • Collections held at 97% plus efficiency while the program grew
  • One question settled with data: schools giving feedback dropped out at nearly the same rate as schools giving none, so the retention effort moved to where it mattered

Takeaway

The case studies on this site show how I think. This is what happened when it shipped: ask the people who left before guessing why they left, build the program the evidence asks for, and put the numbers where leadership can see them without asking, stated conservatively enough to survive an audit.

ccult.fit Product improvement Capstone

The anchored week

Only 34% of new cultpass members build a routine in their first 45 days. The app is not missing features. It is missing the thread between them, and the member is currently the only thing joining them up.

Open prototype
34 → 50%Routine activation, the six month targetEight workouts across four or more weeks, in 45 days
0 of 20Members who ever booked after a push notificationThe finding that killed the obvious solution
₹66 LIncremental renewal revenue per 10,000 member cohort, a yearAt close to zero acquisition cost
6Centres visited, with five manager interviewsPlus 20 surveys and public review mining

The problem

A prepaid promise that quietly cancels itself

cult.fit books revenue the day somebody buys a cultpass, but next year's revenue is decided by whether they show up often enough to renew. That makes the first 45 days the window in which the business is actually won or lost.

The milestone the company tracks is deliberately shaped. Eight workouts in the first 45 days, spread across at least four different weeks. The spread clause is the hard part, because ten workouts crammed into a fortnight still fails. A burst is not a routine. Today only 34% of new members clear it.

Who this is for

New cultpass members aged 25 to 34, in metro cities, with full time desk jobs, either beginners or returning after a gap. They buy on a trigger, usually weight, a health scare, or a feeling that it was time. Then they stall, because they do not know what to do, work eats the evening, and motivation drains without anything noticing.

QWhy does activation matter more than acquisition here?
AThree things compound on the same member. Renewal follows directly from whether a routine formed. Commerce, meaning gear and personal training packs, only attaches to an active member. And centres are fixed cost floors, so profit is utilisation. An activated member does all three. A stalled one does none.
QWhat is in scope?
AProduct only. The research surfaced real operational problems, including class timings that change day to day and peak hour crowding, but I cannot fix a rota from inside an app. Everything proposed here is built from surfaces cult.fit already ships.

The approach · Field research

Led by the people who watch this happen every day

A survey of current members only ever reaches the ones who stayed. The people I most needed to understand had already stopped opening the app. So the primary channel was not the survey. It was sit down interviews with five senior branch and area managers across six centres in Chennai, who watch first 45 day behaviour every single day and can name it.

Alongside that ran a screened survey of 20 active, paused and lapsed members, community and review mining that reaches the churned voices a survey cannot, and a live audit of 20 app screens.

Six findings, and the ones that mattered came from the field

FindingWhat it means for the product

Discipline

I went in believing the primary barrier was the miss spiral, in which one missed session cascades into permanent drop off. The survey did not support it, since 7 of 20 reported returning the next day. But fully churned members are unreachable by a survey of current members, so the hypothesis is inconclusive rather than disproven. I demoted it instead of deleting it, and it re enters below as the explicit thing Reserve Session is built to test.

"cult.fit forces you to change your life to their schedule."

A public review, echoing what the managers described independently

The real problem

New members attend a first workout and never form a routine, because the app never connects its own features into a guided journey. Booking, plans and tracking ship as islands, so the member is the only integration layer, and intent decays feature by feature into silence.

The approach · Prioritisation

Fifteen ideas, pruned to ten, scored down to two

I brainstormed against the barrier rather than against the product, then removed anything cult.fit already ships. What survived was scored on an evidence rubric, in which confidence is set by the quality of the proof behind an idea rather than by how strongly I believed in it.

Why this scoring matters

Smarter notifications is the answer most teams ship first. It scores 1.7 here specifically because the confidence term is doing its job. A rubric that cannot produce that result is a rubric that only ratifies what you already wanted to build.

The solutions

One builds the week. The other protects it.

Neither bet asks for new centres, new trainers or new supply. Both are orchestration of surfaces cult.fit already ships, which is what makes them credible inside a fixed cost business.

Bet 1

The anchored journey

A forty second day zero setup captures four things: preferred slots, comfort level, a micro goal in the member's own words, and how they want to feel afterwards. The journey then leans on those for 45 days.

It sequences what already exists. A class preview before a first time format, one meal suggestion after a hard session, streaks re skinned as weeks kept rather than day counters, progress read against the member's own goal, and an identity arc from Explorer to Regular to Anchored.

The single new idea is drift response. Past day 45 it runs silent until drift: a day 21 recall of why they signed up, a gentler suggestion after a rough session, and complete silence in any week the member is on track.

Bet 2

Reserve session

A lost session never means a lost week. cult.fit's structural advantage, having gym, group class, sport and home under one membership, turned into a rescue ladder that fires when an evening is at risk:

  1. An express version of tonight's session, same centre
  2. A different format in the same slot, so dance or yoga instead of the floor
  3. A sport instead. Badminton, football, pickleball. Different mood, still counts
  4. A home workout, equipment light, capped once a week
  5. Shift the time, keep the day. The calendar updates itself
  6. A same week make up after a miss. One offer within 24 hours, never next Monday

No single format competitor can copy a cross format rescue ladder.

Every drop off archetype is caught by exactly one bet

ArchetypeCaught byMechanism

The sporadic fitterReserve sessionThe at risk evening finally has a fallback that is lighter than the plan and still counts.

Coverage is the point. Two bets, four archetypes, no overlap and no gap. Both of them route into Squad, the accountability mechanism the field identified as the only cue that lands, rather than trying to rebuild it.
